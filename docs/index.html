HookYou think AI shaved four hours off your media planning deck. What it actually did was hand your profit margins straight back to the ad networks. StoryPicture this scenario: A planner prompts an LLM to build a cross-channel media mix across Meta, DV360, and The Trade Desk. In two minutes flat, out comes a clean, balanced budget allocation table. It looks defensible, structured, and perfectly logical. So the agency signs off and pushes the framework live across accounts. ProblemThe hidden problem is what gets stripped out: human variance. It makes automated auction pacing friction-free for them, allowing their systems to steer spend toward inventory that maximizes platform yield rather than client working-media efficiency. Use AI exclusively for data parsing and syntax formatting, never for determining budget splits. 2. Introduce deliberate, manual bidding constraints that break algorithmic predictability across platforms. 3. Treat unique allocation logic as proprietary IP rather than feeding standard planning parameters into third-party models. Subject line: Running ads isn't marketing (grab a chai, let's talk)

Most people think marketing is just flashy Instagram ads and huge discounts.

Picture this: a brand spends lakhs pushing sleek creative assets for an insulated soup flask in coastal Chennai in May, only to wonder why sales are dead.

Nobody bought because nobody needed it there, no matter how pretty the ad looked.

Let’s unpack what marketing actually means before you spend another rupee on paid media.

This week's lesson · Core marketing

What marketing actually is: needs, value and exchange (beyond 'running ads')

Welcome to Issue #1! If you are a student, a fresher, or switching careers into marketing, here is the first truth you must internalize: running ads is not marketing. Running ads—or what we call paid distribution—is merely the megaphone. If what you are whispering into that megaphone is useless, turning up the volume only annoys people faster.

At its core, marketing comes down to three foundational pillars: Needs, Value, and Exchange.

1. Needs and Wants A need is a basic human state of deprivation. You get hungry, you feel lonely, you need safety, or you want to feel respected. A want is the specific way culture and personality shape how you fulfill that need.

For example, when you are hungry and running late for an office standup, your fundamental need is sustenance and speed. Your want might be an idli delivery from a neighborhood cloud kitchen in 10 minutes.

2. Value Value is not just the price tag; it is an equation in the customer’s brain: Perceived Benefits minus Perceived Costs. Costs include actual money, but also time, mental energy, and friction. Benefits include functional utility (it fills your stomach), emotional relief (you won't be stressed about being late), and social signaling (it looks cool).

Consider Zepto or Blinkit delivering groceries in under fifteen minutes. Are they simply selling onions and milk? No. Local kirana stores sell onions cheaper. Customers willingly pay delivery charges because the value is reclaimed time and convenience. Blinkit is not selling groceries; it is selling 45 minutes of your evening back to you.

3. The Exchange Marketing only occurs when an exchange happens. You provide something of value (a product, a service, an experience), and the user voluntarily trades something of value (money, personal attention, data). If either side feels shortchanged, the exchange breaks down, and long-term marketing fails.

Beginner Pitfalls to Avoid - Product-First Bias: Starting with "Look at our cool product features!" instead of "What actual problem does this solve for a real human?" - Confusing Channels with Strategy: Believing that knowing how to toggle campaign switches in Meta Ads Manager or Google Ads makes you a marketer. Tools change every year; human psychology does not. - Ignoring Post-Purchase Reality: Thinking marketing ends when the user hits 'Buy'. If the unboxing experience or product quality falls flat, your retention collapses.

How This Shows Up in a Real Marketing Job When you sit in a media-planning or campaign review meeting, junior executives usually report surface-level metrics: "We achieved a ₹12 Cost Per Click (CPC) on our Meta campaign."

A solid marketer asks the deeper questions: "Who was clicking? Did our messaging match their actual pain point, or did we just attract accidental clicks with clickbait? Did those users actually find enough value on the landing page to convert?"

Whether you are managing programmatic campaigns on DV360 or drafting an email blast, always trace back to first principles: What need are we solving, what value are we proving, and why should the customer make this exchange?
